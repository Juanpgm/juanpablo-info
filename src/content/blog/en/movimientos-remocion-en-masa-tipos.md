In Colombia, with its Andean topography and intense rainfall, mass movements are among the most frequent geological hazards, with major cumulative losses in infrastructure and lives. But "landslide" is a term used far too generically: geotechnics has a precise classification that matters a great deal, because the type of movement determines both how fast it occurs and what mitigation strategy actually applies.

## What a mass movement is

A mass movement is the downslope displacement of a mass of soil, rock, or a mixture of both, driven directly by gravity. What distinguishes one type from another is mainly the displacement mechanism: whether the material falls freely, slides along a defined surface, flows like a viscous fluid, or deforms slowly with no clear rupture surface.

## The main types

**Falls and topples**: occur when blocks of rock or soil detach from a very steep slope or a near-vertical face and descend by free fall, bouncing, or rolling. Typical of fractured rock slopes, their most dangerous trait is speed — they leave practically no reaction time.

**Rotational slides**: material moves along a curved, upward-concave failure surface, producing the movement's characteristic rotation (often with a visible scarp at the crown and back-tilted surfaces within the slide body). Typical of homogeneous clay soils and cut slopes on road projects.

**Translational slides**: material moves along a roughly planar failure surface, frequently coinciding with a weak lithological contact, a structural discontinuity, or the boundary between residual soil and its parent rock. These tend to be faster and travel farther than rotational slides.

**Debris flows**: when material loses cohesion under extreme saturation and behaves like a viscous fluid, channeling through gullies and streambeds with high energy and destructive capacity. These are among the most dangerous movements in Colombian hillside areas because they trigger rapidly during intense rainfall and can travel long distances at high speed.

**Creep**: a slow, nearly imperceptible movement that shows up as progressive tilting of trees, posts, or fences, and gradual ground deformation with no defined rupture surface. It's not usually catastrophic on its own, but it's an early warning sign of instability that can precede a more sudden movement.

## What triggers a mass movement

No slope becomes unstable "for no reason" — there's always a conditioning factor (geology, slope angle, soil type) and a triggering factor that breaks the equilibrium. The most common triggers in the Colombian context are:

1. **Saturation from intense or prolonged rainfall**: water reduces the soil's shear strength by increasing pore pressure and decreasing internal friction between particles.
2. **Seismic activity**: the acceleration induced by an earthquake can exceed a marginal slope's static safety factor, even without prior rain.
3. **Scour**: erosion at the base of a slope from a stream or wave action removes toe support, increasing shear stress in the rest of the mass.
4. **Human intervention**: poorly designed road cuts, overloading a slope crown with construction, or deforestation that removes vegetation cover that helped control infiltration.

Understanding this classification isn't an academic exercise — it directly defines which kind of mitigation work makes sense. A debris flow requires streambed management works and sediment traps; a rotational slide can be stabilized with retaining structures and deep drainage; creep, on the other hand, often only requires continuous monitoring. ## From terrain to data: deformation monitoring

One of the most interesting advances in managing this risk is that today it's possible to monitor slope deformation before a movement turns catastrophic, using geodetic techniques such as satellite InSAR or permanent GNSS stations that detect millimeter-scale displacement over time. That ability to turn scattered geodetic signals into early instability warnings is exactly the kind of bridge between the physical world of terrain and the world of data that draws me in most these days; I wrote about this in more detail in [data analytics in geodesy](/en/blog/analitica-datos-geodesia/).
